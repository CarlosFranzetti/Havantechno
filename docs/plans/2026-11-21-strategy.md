# Strategy after the Stem Crew and Circuit Crew research

Coordinator, 21 November 2026 (ET). Written from six research transcripts (`docs/research/2026-11-21-stem-crew/transcripts/S1-izotope.md`, `S2-separation.md`, `S3-detection.md`; `docs/research/2026-11-21-circuit-crew/transcripts/C1-hardware.md`, `C2-open-emulations.md`, `C3-methods.md`). The transcripts tag every claim as read, search summary or memory; this document carries their conclusions and says where the coordinator checked something itself. No code was written in this round.

## The owner's request

"I want a bunch of research and reasoning on how to approach this further. Eventually I do want what I've been asking but I also want the app to separate the elements into audios stems and I want you research how isotope RX 12 does their analysis and what engines a izotope uses and look into as many RE'd or open source sound engines and stem handling audio. Separately I want you to hunt down the chips and schematics for the different soft synths and see if there are also open source emus if so let's use that. If not let's start reverses engineeering some of it. Improved the audio detection engine and improve my synths so they're closer to arturias methods."

## 1. What the research says, in short

**iZotope and the commercial tools (S1).** RX 12 exists (spring 2026). iZotope names two model families in a keynote summary, Band-Split RNN and BS-RoFormer, both of which have open implementations; it does not say which tool uses which and publishes no training data or scores. Its separation stops at four music classes and dialogue, music and effects. No iZotope tool names an instrument, a tempo or a note, and no commercial tool found turns a record into synth patches; RipX's note-level editing comes nearest. What is worth taking is workflow: stems that sum back to the original with the residual on its own lane, a view with one lane per stem, an assistant that proposes an editable result, classification the user can correct, and draft and final quality.

**Open separation (S2).** HT-Demucs remains a sound default. The strongest newer models (RoFormer variants) mostly come as community weights with no licence statement; the cleanly licensed ones are few. Every drum-kit separator found was trained on acoustic kits and none outputs clap, rim or cowbell. The app can render unlimited electronic material with exact per-voice truth, which is training data the field does not have. One defect in the app's own tool, confirmed by the coordinator in `tools/separate.py` line 174: Demucs is called without float output, so stems are written as 16-bit and rescaled, which by Demucs' own documentation can change the level of one stem against another.

**Detection (S3).** Nothing found identifies a drum-machine or synth model from audio, and no transcriber outputs a 303's slides and accents. The proposal: fix the grid first (an MIT beat and downbeat tracker), then the notes (an Apache-licensed transcriber with a JavaScript port), separate the drum stem into pieces, shortlist candidate instruments, and then fit the app's own instruments to each element by rendering and comparing (analysis by synthesis). S3 supports that idea as the most valuable stage, on two conditions: the grid and notes must be right first, and every threshold must be calibrated on separated stems, not clean solos. A plain search would take 18 to 60 minutes a song by its estimate; with a shortlist, short excerpts and several render pages it estimates one to five minutes.

**Hardware (C1).** Service notes for the TB-303, Juno-106, Juno-60 and TR-909 can be fetched. Their schematic drawings are scanned images the researcher could not see, so no component value has yet been read from a Roland drawing. No public simulator netlist was found for the 303's filter with its accent circuit, the 808 or 909 voices, the Juno's filter chip or its chorus. An emulator's source records checksums for the instruments' ROMs; nothing read grants permission to redistribute the ROM images.

**Open emulations (C2).** Per instrument: for the 303, a diode-ladder filter and output network in JC303 (AGPL-3.0-or-later, calibrated to its author's own circuit simulations, no hardware comparison stated); for the Model D, CA-72's light model (GPL-3.0-or-later), fitted to a circuit model and calibrated against a modern recreation of the Model D, which the coordinator confirmed in CA-72's own notes; for the Juno, parts of KR-106 (GPL-3.0) and a published bucket-brigade chorus model; for the 909, nothing better than what the app uses; for the 808, no open circuit-level voice set.

**Methods (C3).** Arturia publishes goals, not methods: no paper, patent or version history was found for its TAE. The goals it states are free-running, alias-free oscillators with the circuit's waveform curvature and instability, filters modelled from the components with their nonlinearity inside the feedback loop, and comparison against a real unit. The published toolkit that reaches those goals is open, and a circuit simulator (ngspice 42) runs in this sandbox: the coordinator ran a test circuit and got its hand-calculated corner.

## 2. Reasoning

1. **Separation is a commodity; identification and recreation are the product.** Every commercial tool separates; none rebuilds. The app should use the best open separator it can run and spend its own effort where nobody else is: naming each element and rebuilding it with an instrument and a patch.

2. **The two halves of the request are one system.** The instruments are also the yardstick of the detection engine: to decide that a bass line is a 303, the app renders its 303 against it and measures the difference. A more faithful 303 therefore fits a real 303 line better and a wrong instrument worse. Improving the synths improves detection, and the detection engine's fitting loop is a ready-made test of the synths.

3. **One working loop for both halves: truth, model, bench, listen.** For instruments the truth is a circuit simulation of the schematic plus published measurements; for detection it is the app's own renders, with labels, pushed through the separator. In both cases a script compares and exits non-zero when the model drifts, and a person listens at the end. This is the Ear Crew's loop, which is the only method on this project that has demonstrably converged.

4. **Use open work where it exists, and check it against our own reference.** C2 would port existing code; C3 would derive models from the schematic. These are not rivals. Porting is days and gets a better sound soon; our own simulation of the same block is the independent check that tells us whether the ported code (or our own) is right. Where no open model exists (the 303's accent interaction with a nonlinear filter, the 808 and 909 voices, the Juno chorus's real parts), the simulation is the reverse engineering.

5. **"Closer to Arturia's methods" means their stated goals, reached with published methods.** It cannot mean matching their plug-ins: their output is not a reference for the hardware, and their internals are not public. The practical gap between the app's engine and those goals is specific: the filters' nonlinearity is outside the loop or guessed, the Juno voice is not oversampled, the chorus is an ideal delay, voices are identical, and waveforms are ideal shapes.

6. **What a simulation cannot do.** It confirms a circuit's topology and nominal part values. It cannot stand in for one particular unit, its aged parts or its factory trim, and real units differ from each other by more than the tolerances proposed here. Nobody on this project has heard a real unit next to the app, and the Model D's knob laws follow a recreation. A recording of a real instrument with its knob positions written down would be worth more than any further simulation.

## 3. Plan, in stages (each step ends with a check that can fail)

**Stage A, small fixes with immediate effect.**
- A1. `tools/separate.py`: float output without rescaling, a residual file (mix minus the sum of stems) with its level, model and weights hashes and licence strings in the sidecar. Check: stems plus residual equal the mix to the sample on a labelled render; scores compared with the present 24.0 / 17.7 / 19.4 dB.
- A2. Juno: oversample the voice loop. Check: aliasing measured on high notes and bright PWM before and after; CPU measured on a live context.
- A3. Seeded per-voice variance and drift on the Juno, tuning error on the 303. Check: two renders with the same seed identical; spread within stated tolerances.

**Stage B, the reverse-engineering loop, 303 first (the owner's focus).**
- B1. View the 303 service notes' drawings page by page (documents fetched to the scratchpad, never the repository), read every part value in the filter, its coupling capacitors, the resonance path, the accent network and the envelope, and write simulator decks with each part's designator. Check: hand-calculated corners; two readers agree on every value, disagreements listed.
- B2. Reference tables from the decks (frequency response against cutoff and resonance, step, sweep, distortion against level, the accent voltage over consecutive accents) stored as data; a test input on the bench; today's engine measured against them first, to record the present error.
- B3. Port JC303's diode-ladder filter and output network into the worklet behind an accuracy setting (the present filter stays as the draft tier), and measure it against B2. Keep Open303 for the oscillator, envelopes, accent and slide. Check: bench within ±0.5 dB passband, ±1 dB at the resonance peak, ±2 % peak frequency (C3's proposed tolerances); CPU on a live context; before and after demos.
- B4. Juno chorus as a bucket-brigade model after the published paper, with the Juno-60's values. Check: delay sweep against the measured 1.66 to 5.35 ms; stereo width; noise level marked as a guess.
- B5. Model D: port CA-72's light model block by block (laws, oscillators, the oversampled ladder, contours, voice). Check: the existing reference pitches and the passband error (2.5 dB today near the corner).

**Stage C, detection, second version.**
- C1. Fitting by synthesis on clean renders first: shortlist, short excerpt, discrete switches enumerated, 303 slides and accents decided per step by rendering. Check: instrument right on 95 % of the app's own labelled solos; slides 19 of 20 on the lines already measured; parameter error reported with an interval, never a bare number.
- C2. Grid: the beat and downbeat tracker in the Python sidecar, cross-checked against the present tempo code. Check: tempo and bar 1 on renders at 98, 126 and 174 BPM and on a section without drums.
- C3. Notes: the transcriber for chords and bass lines. Check: notes and lengths on the labelled lines; chords no longer read as one note.
- C4. Drum stem into pieces: score the two public models and the present band split on the app's own 808 and 909 grooves, where each voice's truth exists; adopt one only if it beats the band split by a margin set beforehand.
- C5. The same fits through the separator on full labelled songs, to set confidence thresholds; then the interface: one lane per stem with the residual, and a choice between the top two hypotheses by ear, stored as a label.

**Stage D, later.** A drum-piece separator trained on the app's own renders (the only route to clap, rim and cowbell outputs, and to weights with terms of our own; needs a GPU this sandbox does not have); 808 and 909 voices as circuit models; the 909's real hats and cymbals from a ROM file the owner supplies, verified by checksum and never stored in the repository; isolation by example ("more like this sound").

Stages A, B and C touch different files and can run at the same time. The order of value, by the coordinator's judgement: A1, then B1 to B3 and C1 together, then A2 and B4, then C2 to C5, then B5.

## 4. Licence position

For an app that is AGPL-3.0-or-later, not sold, and used by the owner and a few friends: MIT, BSD, ISC, Apache-2.0, GPL-3.0 and AGPL-3.0 code can be ported with a row in `THIRD_PARTY.md` (GPL-3.0 without "or later", as KR-106 states it, ties that part to version 3). Model weights with non-commercial terms or with no stated licence are never committed, mirrored or bundled: they are optional downloads on the user's machine, labelled as such, with their source recorded. Code with no licence statement is not copied; its published numbers may be cited. ROM images and manuals stay out of the repository. The coordinator is not a lawyer; where the transcripts mark a licence question as unsettled (training data reaching weights; share-alike weights), this plan avoids depending on the answer.

## 5. What is not known

- Whether public drum-piece separators work on electronic kits at all: unmeasured (C4 measures it).
- Whether weights trained on the app's own renders generalise to real records: unproven.
- CPU cost of the heavier models in a browser on a phone: no measurement exists.
- Component values of every Roland circuit: unread until B1.
- How any of the instruments sounds next to a real one: nobody has listened, and no recording of a real unit is in hand.
- The research itself: the owner's Firecrawl credits ran out part-way, so parts of S2, S3 and C3 rest on search summaries and memory; each transcript lists which.
